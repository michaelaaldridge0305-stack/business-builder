# When Someone Dies in England: A Calm Practical Guide

**Ikhaya Designs GB — working manuscript**  
**Version:** 2 October 2026  
**Scope:** England  
**Status:** Evidence-backed working manuscript for design and final source QA

> This guide is designed to reduce the practical load after a death. You do not need to do everything at once. Start with the first few steps, keep a simple record of what you have done, and come back to later sections when you are ready.

## Important note

This is practical guidance, not legal, tax or financial advice. Estates vary. If the estate is complex, disputed, insolvent, includes trusts or overseas assets, or you are unsure of your responsibilities, consider getting professional advice.

Government processes and benefit rules can change. The final published guide should direct readers to GOV.UK for current rules.

---

# 1. The first few days

## What matters first

In the first few days, focus on what is genuinely time-sensitive:

- wait for the medical examiner process to be completed where applicable;
- register the death when you are told you can do so;
- locate any will, funeral wishes, prepaid funeral plan or relevant insurance;
- begin thinking about funeral arrangements;
- keep important papers together in one place.

You do not need to start closing every account immediately.

### Registering the death in England

After a death, the doctor confirms the death and contacts the medical examiner. The medical examiner checks the cause of death. Their office will then tell you when you can register the death.

In England, the death should normally be registered within **5 days of receiving confirmation that you can register it**, including weekends and bank holidays. If you need longer, contact the register office.

If the death has been reported to a coroner, the process can be different.

After registration, you will receive the certificate needed for burial or cremation, often called the **green form**. This is provided to the funeral director, crematorium or burial authority before the funeral can take place.

**Source:** GOV.UK, “What to do after someone dies: Register the death”  
https://www.gov.uk/after-a-death/register-the-death

### Practical record

Keep a note of:

- date the death was registered;
- register office;
- number of death certificates ordered;
- Tell Us Once reference number, if provided;
- funeral director or other funeral contact;
- important calls made and reference numbers.

# 2. Tell government departments

## Tell Us Once

Tell Us Once allows you to report a death to most government organisations in one go.

A registrar will usually explain the service when you register the death. They may complete it with you or give you a unique reference number to use online or by phone.

The reference number must normally be used within **28 days**.

Tell Us Once can notify organisations including HMRC, DWP, the Passport Office, DVLA and the local council. It also notifies certain public-sector pension schemes.

Tell Us Once does **not** replace every notification you may need to make. You may still need to contact:

- banks and building societies;
- mortgage providers;
- insurance companies;
- utility providers;
- landlords or housing associations;
- employers;
- private or workplace pension providers not covered by the service;
- subscription and membership providers.

**Source:** GOV.UK, “Tell Us Once”  
https://www.gov.uk/after-a-death/organisations-you-need-to-contact-and-tell-us-once

### If Tell Us Once cannot be used

You will need to contact relevant government organisations individually. GOV.UK provides specific guidance for this situation.

**Source:** GOV.UK, “Report the death without a Tell Us Once reference number”  
https://www.gov.uk/after-a-death/report-without-tell-us-once

# 3. Arranging the funeral

A funeral can usually take place after the death has been registered, unless the death has been referred to a coroner.

You can use a funeral director or arrange the funeral yourself.

Before committing to costs, check whether the person who died had:

- written funeral wishes;
- a prepaid funeral plan;
- life insurance;
- money specifically set aside for funeral costs.

Funeral wishes can be important to the family, but do not assume every written wish is legally binding. Where there is disagreement or uncertainty, seek appropriate advice.

**Source:** GOV.UK, “Arrange the funeral”  
https://www.gov.uk/after-a-death/arrange-the-funeral

## Help with funeral costs

A **Funeral Expenses Payment** may be available if the person arranging the funeral receives certain qualifying benefits and meets the other eligibility rules.

A claim must normally be made within **6 months of the funeral**. A claim can be made before the funeral if there is an invoice or signed contract from the funeral director; an estimate is not enough.

Eligibility depends on circumstances, so do not assume that receiving a benefit automatically guarantees payment.

**Sources:**  
https://www.gov.uk/funeral-payments/eligibility  
https://www.gov.uk/funeral-payments/how-to-claim

# 4. Find the will and identify who is responsible for the estate

Look for the most recent valid will.

Useful places to check include:

- the person’s home and important-paper files;
- their solicitor;
- their bank or professional adviser;
- information held by family members.

The will may name one or more **executors**. Executors are responsible for administering the estate.

If there is no valid will, the estate is dealt with under the rules of intestacy and the person entitled to administer it may need to apply for letters of administration.

Do not distribute possessions or money simply because family members agree informally. First establish who has legal authority to deal with the estate and whether probate or another grant is required.

**Primary starting point:** GOV.UK probate and estate guidance  
https://www.gov.uk/probate-estate

# 5. Build an estate inventory

Before trying to settle the estate, create a clear picture of what exists.

## Assets

Record, where relevant:

- current and savings accounts;
- cash;
- investments and shares;
- property and land;
- vehicles;
- business interests;
- valuable personal possessions;
- money owed to the person;
- life insurance or policies payable to the estate.

For each item, record the organisation, account/reference number where safe to do so, approximate value at the date of death, and whether the organisation has been notified.

## Liabilities

Record:

- mortgages;
- loans;
- credit cards;
- overdrafts;
- unpaid household bills;
- tax due;
- benefit overpayments;
- other money owed.

Do not personally promise to pay the deceased person’s debts from your own money merely because a creditor contacts you. Debts are normally dealt with as part of administering the estate. Complex or insolvent estates need particular care.

## Useful documents

Gather:

- will and codicils;
- death certificates;
- bank and investment statements;
- pension information;
- property documents;
- insurance policies;
- tax records;
- loan and credit agreements;
- recent bills;
- records of gifts where relevant to inheritance-tax reporting;
- funeral invoices and estate-administration expenses.

# 6. Work out whether probate is needed

“Probate” is often used as a general term for obtaining legal authority to deal with an estate.

Whether a grant is needed depends on the assets and how they were owned. Some organisations may release smaller balances without a grant, while property or larger assets may require one.

If a valid will names executors, they may apply for a **grant of probate**. If there is no will, the appropriate person may apply for **letters of administration**.

Check the current GOV.UK probate process before applying, including any inheritance-tax steps that need to happen first.

**Source:** GOV.UK, probate and estate guidance  
https://www.gov.uk/probate-estate

---

# 7. Deal with debts and tax before distributing the estate

The person administering the estate is responsible for settling debts and taxes from the estate before distributing what remains.

This can include:

- unpaid bills;
- personal taxes due up to the date of death;
- tax refunds due to the estate;
- a final Self Assessment return where required;
- repayment of overpaid benefits;
- Inheritance Tax where applicable;
- tax on income generated by the estate during administration.

HMRC may need information about final salary or pension payments.

GOV.UK advises personal representatives to consider placing a notice in **The Gazette** giving creditors **2 months** to make a claim. Distributing the estate too early can expose a personal representative to personal liability if later debts cannot be paid.

Do not distribute the estate until you are satisfied that debts, taxes and administration costs have been dealt with or sufficient money has been retained for them.

**Source:** GOV.UK, “Settling debts and taxes”  
https://www.gov.uk/probate-estate/settling-debts-and-taxes

---

# 8. Keep estate records

Estate administration is easier when decisions and transactions are recorded as they happen.

Keep a simple log containing:

- money received by the estate;
- bills and debts paid;
- funeral and administration expenses;
- assets sold;
- tax paid or refunded;
- distributions to beneficiaries;
- dates, reference numbers and copies of important correspondence.

Use a dedicated folder, spreadsheet or notebook rather than relying on memory.

When administration is complete, final estate accounts can be prepared. GOV.UK says these should be approved and signed by the main beneficiaries.

**Source:** GOV.UK, “Distributing the estate”  
https://www.gov.uk/probate-estate/distribute-the-estate

# 9. Check whether you can claim Bereavement Support Payment

If your partner has died, you may be eligible for **Bereavement Support Payment**.

Eligibility depends on factors including your relationship, age and your partner’s National Insurance record or circumstances of death.

The payment is not means-tested.

Timing matters. A claim should normally be made within **3 months** of the death to receive the full amount available. Claims can usually still be made later, up to **21 months**, but the amount may be reduced.

Current maximum rates depend on circumstances. Because rates and eligibility can change, the finished guide should direct readers to GOV.UK rather than relying only on printed figures.

**Sources:**  
https://www.gov.uk/bereavement-support-payment/eligibility  
https://www.gov.uk/bereavement-support-payment/how-to-claim  
https://www.gov.uk/bereavement-support-payment/what-youll-get

# 10. Things people often forget

Once the urgent tasks are under control, work through this list slowly.

Consider whether you need to contact or update:

- employer;
- private pension providers;
- banks and building societies;
- mortgage or landlord;
- home, car, life and other insurers;
- gas, electricity and water;
- phone and broadband;
- mobile provider;
- streaming and subscription services;
- clubs and memberships;
- professional bodies;
- charities receiving regular donations;
- social-media and online accounts;
- email accounts and cloud storage;
- loyalty schemes;
- vehicle ownership and insurance;
- property records;
- pets’ registration, insurance or care arrangements.

Not every item will apply. Cross out what does not matter.

# 11. A calmer way to organise the admin

Grief can make concentration and memory unreliable. Build the system around that reality rather than expecting yourself to remember everything.

## Use four simple lists

### Do now
Time-sensitive tasks only.

### Do next
Important tasks that can wait until the immediate arrangements are settled.

### Waiting for
Anything dependent on another person or organisation.

### Done
Keep completed items visible. It prevents duplicate calls and gives you a record of progress.

## For every call, record

- date;
- organisation;
- person spoken to;
- reference number;
- what was agreed;
- what happens next;
- expected timescale.

You are allowed to stop after one or two difficult calls. Administrative progress does not need to happen in one sitting.

---

# 12. Looking after yourself while doing the practical work

There is no correct timetable for grief.

Practical tasks can sometimes provide structure, but they can also be exhausting. Where possible:

- ask someone you trust to sit with you while you make calls;
- divide tasks rather than carrying everything alone;
- write things down;
- avoid making unnecessary major decisions while overwhelmed;
- take breaks after difficult conversations;
- seek support if grief is becoming difficult to manage alone.

Your local council may be able to direct you to bereavement services and local support.

**Source:** GOV.UK, local bereavement services  
https://www.gov.uk/find-bereavement-services-from-council

If you are struggling with your mental health or feel unable to cope safely, seek appropriate urgent support through NHS services or emergency services.

## Final-design QA notes

- Keep England scope explicit on cover and disclaimer.
- Do not imply Tell Us Once contacts private companies.
- Do not imply every estate requires probate.
- Do not present Funeral Expenses Payment as automatic.
- Preserve timing distinctions: death registration timing begins after confirmation to register; Tell Us Once reference is normally used within 28 days; Funeral Expenses Payment within 6 months of funeral; Bereavement Support Payment timing affects entitlement.
- Re-check all time limits and benefit figures immediately before publication.
- Keep legal/tax caveat visible but not alarmist.
- Layout should reduce cognitive load: short sections, checkboxes, generous spacing, no decorative clutter.
